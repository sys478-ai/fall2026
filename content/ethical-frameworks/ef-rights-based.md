## The Core Idea

Rights-based ethics asks whether a system respects protections that people are entitled to claim. Rights set limits on what can be justified by good outcomes alone.

Rights-based ethics emphasizes three ideas:

1. **People have protected claims.** Privacy, due process, equal treatment, freedom from discrimination, and other rights protect people against certain forms of treatment.
2. **Rights set limits on outcomes.** A system does not become acceptable simply because it is efficient or produces an overall benefit.
3. **Rights require institutions and remedies.** A protection matters in practice only when people can know about it, assert it, and seek redress when it is violated.

In AI ethics, this means asking whether a system respects the protections people should be able to claim regardless of its outcomes.

  <p class="diagnostic-question-eyebrow">Diagnostic Question</p>
  <p class="diagnostic-question-text">Does this system violate a protection that people are entitled to regardless of the outcomes it produces?</p>
</div>

## How This Framework Is Often Applied

This framework is often used in law, public policy, and governance to identify protections that systems must respect. In AI, it appears in debates about privacy, discrimination, surveillance, explanation, due process, and the ability to challenge an automated decision. It is especially important when systems affect access to employment, housing, education, healthcare, public benefits, or freedom of movement.

Rights-based analysis asks more than whether a system has a good average outcome. It asks what rights are at stake, whether affected people know a decision is being made about them, and whether they have a meaningful way to contest or remedy a violation. Better technical performance may reduce harm, but it does not by itself answer whether a protected right has been respected.

## Strengths and Weaknesses

### What This Lens Reveals

Rights-based ethics is especially strong at identifying questions of:

- **Protected limits** – when some treatment is unacceptable even if it produces a useful result.
- **Privacy and autonomy** – whether people can control information about themselves and make decisions without unjustified intrusion.
- **Equal treatment and due process** – whether people are protected from discrimination and can understand or challenge consequential decisions.
- **Institutional accountability** – what legal protections, remedies, and enforcement mechanisms exist in practice.

In AI ethics, this lens is useful for identifying non-negotiable protections that should not be traded away for efficiency, accuracy, or aggregate benefit.

### What It Can Miss

Rights-based ethics is less well suited to questions about:

- **Conflicting rights** – how to decide when one person's rights appear to conflict with another's.
- **Scope** – which rights exist, who holds them, and what exactly they require in a new situation.
- **Collective goods and tradeoffs** – how to weigh a protected claim against broader social needs when no clear violation is present.
- **Practical access** – formal rights may provide little protection when people lack information, resources, or power to assert them.

Rights frameworks provide a moral and legal floor, but they do not settle every question about fair outcomes, care, or institutional design.

  <p><strong>John Locke</strong> (1632–1704) provided the foundational account of natural rights – to life, liberty, and property – that cannot be legitimately overridden by governments. His <em>Two Treatises of Government</em> (1689) established rights as pre-political and inalienable, a framework that shaped liberal political thought and the language of human rights declarations.</p>
</div>

<div class="thinker-entry">
  <div class="thinker-avatar-wrap">
    <img class="thinker-avatar" src="/fall2026/images/ethics-field-guide/ethics/roosevelt.jpg" alt="Portrait of Eleanor Roosevelt">
    <span class="thinker-avatar-caption">Public domain</span>
  </div>
  <p><strong>Eleanor Roosevelt</strong> (1884–1962) chaired the committee that drafted the <em>Universal Declaration of Human Rights</em> (1948) and played a leading role in building support for it, translating rights philosophy into international law and helping establish the framework that still shapes global human rights discourse.</p>
</div>

<div class="thinker-entry">
  <div class="thinker-avatar-wrap">

  </div>
  <p><strong>Ronald Dworkin</strong> (1931–2013) developed one of the most influential contemporary rights theories in <em>Taking Rights Seriously</em> (1977), arguing that rights function as "trumps" over aggregate welfare calculations – a direct challenge to utilitarian reasoning in law and policy.</p>
</div>

<div class="thinker-entry">

  <p><strong>Martha Nussbaum</strong> (1947–) developed the capabilities approach – a closely related framework that focuses not only on formal rights, but on what people are actually able to do and be. Her <em>Creating Capabilities</em> (2011) is especially useful for evaluating systems that formally respect rights while undermining the conditions for their exercise.</p>
</div>
