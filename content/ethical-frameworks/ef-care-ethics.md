## The Core Idea

Care ethics asks what people owe one another within relationships of dependency, trust, and responsibility. It begins with the particular people and relationships involved, rather than treating context as something to abstract away.

Care ethics emphasizes three ideas:

1. **Relationships matter.** Moral responsibilities arise from the relationships through which people depend on, trust, and affect one another.
2. **Context and particular needs matter.** A fair response may require understanding a person's specific situation rather than treating them as an interchangeable case.
3. **Care requires accountability.** It is not enough for a system to appear responsive; someone must be responsible for noticing needs and responding when harm occurs.

In AI ethics, this means asking whether a system supports or erodes the relationships of care and trust that people depend on.

## How This Framework Is Often Applied

This framework is often used to examine healthcare, education, social services, child welfare, and other settings where people depend on continuing relationships of support. In AI, it asks what happens when automated systems replace, reshape, or intensify human judgment, discretion, and responsiveness.

Care ethics also draws attention to the distribution of care work and responsibility. A system may present itself as supportive while shifting more monitoring, documentation, or repair work onto teachers, caseworkers, patients, families, or other people with little power. It asks whether automation strengthens accountable care or merely simulates attentiveness while leaving no one responsible for responding to a person's needs.

## Strengths and Weaknesses

### What This Lens Reveals

Care ethics is especially strong at identifying questions of:

- **Relationships** – how systems affect trust, dependency, and responsibility between people.
- **Vulnerability** – whose needs require attention and who bears the consequences when a system fails.
- **Context** – when uniform rules or classifications overlook a person's particular situation.
- **Care work and accountability** – who does the work of noticing needs, repairing harm, and responding to people.

In AI ethics, this lens is especially useful when systems intervene in relationships that previously depended on human judgment and responsiveness.

### What It Can Miss

Care ethics is less well suited to questions about:

- **Fairness across strangers** – how to distribute resources or obligations among people who have no existing relationship.
- **Clear universal rules** – when a decision needs a consistent principle that applies across many cases.
- **Scale** – how to make decisions that affect large populations or institutions.
- **Power within relationships** – caring relationships can be unequal, coercive, or paternalistic rather than automatically good.

Care ethics resists easy formalization. That is a useful warning against treating people as generic cases, but it can make the framework harder to translate into rules, policies, or automated systems.

  <p><strong>Carol Gilligan</strong> (1936–) launched care ethics as a field in <em>In a Different Voice</em> (1982), arguing that dominant theories of moral development – particularly Lawrence Kohlberg's – were built on male-centered conceptions of justice and failed to account for an ethics grounded in relationships and responsiveness. Her work reframed what had been seen as a developmental deficit as an alternative moral voice.</p>
</div>

  </div>
  <p><strong>Nel Noddings</strong> (1929–2022) developed care ethics as a systematic ethical theory in <em>Caring: A Feminine Approach to Ethics and Moral Education</em> (1984), grounding it in the natural caring found in close relationships and asking how that caring could be extended and sustained.</p>
</div>

  <p><strong>Virginia Held</strong> (1929–) extended care ethics beyond personal relationships into political and social theory in <em>The Ethics of Care</em> (2006), arguing that care should replace contract and market as the dominant organizing metaphor for social life.</p>
</div>

  <p><strong>Joan Tronto</strong> (1952–) brought care ethics explicitly into political theory in <em>Moral Boundaries</em> (1993), analyzing how care work is systematically devalued and showing that who does care work – and who is exempted from it – is a question of power, not just personal virtue.</p>
</div>
