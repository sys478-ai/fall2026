## 1. Discussion: Ezra Klein & Sherry Turkle Interview

{% collapsible closed %}
### 1.1. Why Do We Become Attached to Machines?

> "I mean, *[interviews with people using bots for emotional support]* were filled with conversation about the people in their lives who didn’t listen, and how this was kind of a balm, a salve, a place where you knew that someone would.
> 
> So I think that you’ve put your finger on something exactly right, that there’s two sides to this story. I call it technological affordance and human vulnerability. It’s what the machine is putting out – in this case, a really very poor conversation – but human vulnerability was what the people weren’t getting in the rest of their lives."
>
> – Sherry Turkle (p. 6)

#### Discussion Questions

1. Why might someone prefer talking to a chatbot over another person?
2. Does knowing that a chatbot isn't human change how we relate to it?

{% collapsible closed %}
### 1.2. How do AI companions tap into our need to care for others?

> ...and I came, after years of studying *[robot toys for children and the elderly]* and really interviewing the old people with their Paros and the young people with their Furbys and their Tamagotchis, I came to this formulation that we nurture what we love, but we love what we nurture. In other words, when these objects asked for nurturance, people glommed onto that.
>
> They did what the Eliza program didn’t, and this is where they had something very dramatically over the Eliza program. The Eliza program pretended to know what you were saying in an affective way, but it never said: “I care for you. Teach me. I love you. I’m yours. I’m your person.” And these machines started to move in that direction.
>
> – Sherry Turkle (pp. 7-8)

#### Discussion Questions

1. Why do we become attached to things we care for, even when they cannot care for us in return?
2. Can caring for an AI companion be meaningful, even if that care isn't reciprocated?
3. How might companies design AI companions to encourage emotional attachment? Where should we draw the line between companionship and manipulation?

{% collapsible closed %}
### 1.3. Can AI provide the benefits of a caring relationship without actually caring about us?

#### Ezra Klein – The Value of AI as a Social Coach
> “And I didn’t really have anybody I could ask to help me better understand the social world I was part of.”
>
> – Ezra Klein (p. 31)

Klein reflects on his struggles making friends as a teenager. He imagines two possibilities: AI could have become a substitute for human relationships, or it could have helped him understand and navigate those relationships.

{% collapsible closed %}
### 1.4. AI Companionship: Who Benefits, and at What Cost?

> “Social media came for our attention and chatbots come for our capacity for attachment.”
>
> – Sherry Turkle (p. 24)

> “I think that you can only address this problem by working on community in the real world and on politics in the real world.”
>
> – Sherry Turkle (p. 36)

#### Discussion Questions

1. How might companies profit from our emotional attachment to AI companions? How could these incentives shape their design?
2. If AI companions make lonely people feel better, is that enough? Are they solving loneliness or masking a larger social problem?
3. Can AI companions be designed to strengthen human relationships rather than replace them? What incentives or policies would make that possible?

{:.mt-4}

- Check: Does your diagram help explain why things happen, not just what happens?
