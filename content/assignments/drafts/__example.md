### Biological
* **Explains:** The skill as fine motor control – training my tongue to vibrate when making the "R" sound, a movement most English speakers never need and have to build from nothing.
* **Makes important:** That this is a trainable motor skill, not a fixed trait. The "you're either born with it or not" belief turns out to be wrong – repeated daily practice is what let my body eventually find the movement.
* **Leaves out:** Why the effort felt worth sustaining every single day for as long as it took. Motor learning alone doesn't explain persistence.

## Reflection Paragraph (400 words)

The lens that changed my thinking the most was the socio-political one. Going in, I would have said the hard part of rolling my Rs was purely physical – my tongue just didn't know how to do the movement, and I had to train it the way you'd train any muscle. The biological lens backs that up: this is fine motor control, the tongue learning to vibrate against the alveolar ridge, a movement English never asks for. But that lens doesn't explain why I kept going. Motor learning alone doesn't account for showing up to that practice every single day on the walk home, for months, with no teacher checking in and no grade attached. The socio-political lens filled in that gap. I wasn't practicing because I loved phonetics – I was practicing because I did not want to be the "lame gringa" with a valley girl accent, in a school where half the students spoke Spanish and accent was a visible marker of who belonged and who didn't. Once I saw that, I realized the walking-home practice wasn't really about the sound at all. It was about not wanting to be read as an outsider in a place where that read had real social consequences.

What's interesting is what both lenses struggled with together: neither one explains why "you can't learn to roll your Rs, you're born with it" is such a persistent myth. The biological lens should be the one to debunk it – motor skills are trainable, full stop – but knowing that didn't stop me from believing the myth for years before I tested it myself. The socio-political lens explains why I wanted to try, but not why the myth existed in the first place, or why it took direct experience to overturn a belief that biology could have corrected on its own.

That gap raises a real question for AI systems. When people say a model "just doesn't get" sarcasm, or humor, or common sense, are they making the same mistake I made about my own tongue – treating something as a fixed limitation when it might actually be a trainable capability that hasn't been sufficiently practiced or exposed to the right conditions yet? And if that's true, what would actually distinguish a genuinely fixed limit in a system from one that's just undertrained? I don't think either of my two lenses gives me a clean answer – but noticing that both of them go quiet at exactly the same spot feels like the more honest place to end than pretending one lens had it all along.
