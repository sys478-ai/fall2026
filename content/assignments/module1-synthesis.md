A key concept in artificial intelligence is figuring out how to teach a machine to do useful things – and much of the language we use for that (learning, training, feedback) is borrowed directly from how humans learn. Whether that borrowing actually holds up matters: if it doesn't, we risk giving machines credit (or blame) for things they don't actually do – trusting a system's "judgment" the way we'd trust a person's, when what's happening underneath may work nothing like human learning at all. Before we can ask that question well, we need practice actually looking closely at how learning happens in our own lives.

As we've learned, there are many ways of explaining how learning happens, depending on what you're attending to and what you're treating as "background context." This assignment asks you to practice that kind of analysis by reflecting on an experience you've had in your own life.

> **Goal:** This homework is meant to help you practice the main habit of Module 1: using more than one lens to understand the same phenomenon, and noticing that each lens reveals some things while obscuring others. You do not need to summarize every reading or theorist from the module. Focus on one concrete example and use it to show that you can compare perspectives clearly.


Choose one example of learning, judgment, or behavior and analyze it through two Module 1 lenses.

## 1. Choose Your Example

Choose one ordinary example of learning, judgment, or behavior from your own experience or from everyday life.

Possible examples include:

* learning a skill
* forming a habit
* picking up a social norm
* changing your mind about something
* learning from another person
* learning through repetition, feedback, or failure

## 2. Choose Two Lenses

Analyze your example through **two** of the Module 1 lenses:

* biological
* psychological
* sociocultural
* socio-political

Pick the two lenses that fit your example best.

### What does it mean to "apply a lens"?

A lens is a set of questions a discipline asks by habit, and a set of things it assumes matter most. Applying a lens means temporarily thinking like a specialist in that field – not describing the lens in the abstract, but actually using it to explain *your* example.

Try this: apply each perspective to your example and ask "How did this happen?" How would each one explain it, using your specific example?


{: .table-simple}
| Perspective | What it points to |
|---|---|
| **Biological** (neuroscience / neurobiology) | What's happening in the body or brain – neural pathways strengthening with repetition, sensory input (what you see, hear, feel) and motor output (movement, speech) getting wired together, reflexes, muscle memory, and the nervous system adapting over time |
| **Psychological** | How the mind builds and revises a skill or understanding – reinforcement and feedback shaping behavior (Skinner), or existing mental models getting adjusted as new experience doesn't fit (Piaget) – alongside motivation, confidence, and how failure or success changes what you try next |
| **Sociocultural** | How culture, relationships, and community shape learning – guidance and interaction with others (Vygotsky), the knowledge and skills a learner brings from their household or community (Moll) |
| **Socio-political** | How power and structure shape learning – who enforces norms, who bears the cost of not fitting in, and whose knowledge or ways of learning get valued or dismissed (Freire) |

If your answer to "what does this lens explain" sounds like a textbook definition of the lens rather than a sentence about your specific example, go back and make it more concrete.

## 3. Submission: 3 Parts

### 3.1. Slide deck (4 slides)
Keep it simple -- don't overthink it.

{: .box }
**Slide 1 – Title + Example**
* Title (e.g. "How I learned to ___")
* An image or visual representing your example

{: .box }
**Slide 2 – Lens 1**
For your first lens, answer:
1. What does this lens help explain? (What mechanism, process, or cause does it point to?)
2. What does this lens make important? (What does it treat as the real story?)
3. What does this lens leave out or explain less well? (What happened that this lens can't account for?)

{: .box }
**Slide 3 – Lens 2**
Same three questions, applied to your second lens.

{: .box }
**Slide 4 – Reflection**
1. Which lens changed how you thought about the example most? (Name the shift, not just which lens you liked.)
2. Pick one specific thing your two lenses disagreed about, or one thing both lenses struggled to explain. What question does that raise about whether AI systems face the same difficulty? (This should follow from the thing you just named – not a general AI question you could've asked without doing this analysis.)

Keep lens slides concise – short phrases or 1-2 sentences per question. The reflection slide can be bullets too.

### 3.2. Short Reflection paragraph (200–400 words)

Write up your answers to the two reflection questions above as a short written paragraph – actual sentences, not bullet fragments. This is where the real synthesis happens.

### 3.3. Be ready to share in class

Come to class ready to walk a peer through your slides and reflection.

## Before You Submit, Check

- Each lens slide has a *specific* detail from your example, not a generic description of what the lens is
- Your "leaves out" answers are honest – you're testing the lens, not defending it
- Your reflection question is clearly connected to the disagreement or gap you named, not a fresh AI thought
