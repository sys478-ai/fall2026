## What You're Doing

Move from collecting information to interpreting it. Use the course's **AI system analysis** questions to explain how the technology's design interacts with human choices, institutions, and social values:

- **Purpose:** what is the system supposed to do?
- **Mechanism:** what goes in, what is learned or calculated, and what comes out?
- **Decision:** how does the output become consequential?
- **People and institutions:** who builds, owns, deploys, regulates, uses, or experiences it?
- **Claims:** what is descriptive, what is normative, and what is political or institutional?
- **Lenses:** what does each relevant lens reveal, and what does it leave out?
- **Uncertainty:** what do we not know?

By this date you should have a **defensible argument**, even if it is tentative. After this, the work is about communicating it effectively.

## What To Submit

1. **Your central argument**, in one or two sentences.
1. **A systems diagram** showing important inputs, processes, outputs, and feedback loops. A hand-drawn diagram is fine as long as you can explain it.
1. **Two or three findings that support your argument.**
1. **An explanation of who benefits, who may be harmed, and what could be different.** Cover at least three stakeholder groups and at least two consequential design choices.
1. **One recommendation or question for collective action**, grounded in your analysis.

Submit one document per team on Canvas by the due date shown above.

### Tips
- Arguments can be provisional. Revising your claim as evidence changes is part of good inquiry.
- You do not need to argue that your system causes harm or needs regulation (although you certainly can). A thoughtful conclusion might be that the evidence is inconclusive, the benefits depend on context, or a proposed fix addresses the wrong problem.
- Your exhibit should not simply argue that AI is good or bad. It should help visitors see **why** outcomes happen, **who has the power** to shape them, and **what choices are possible**.
