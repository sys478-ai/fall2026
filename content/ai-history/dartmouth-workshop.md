In the summer of 1956, John McCarthy (then at Dartmouth), Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester (IBM), and Claude Shannon (Bell Labs) organize a two-month workshop that gives the field its name. Their 1955 funding proposal states the founding premise plainly: "every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it." They ask the Rockefeller Foundation for funding on the assumption that "a significant advance can be made" if a select group of scientists works on the problem together for a summer.

The workshop does not produce the significant advance it promises. It produces something more durable: a name for the field, a founding cohort of researchers who go on to define its early decades, and a template for how AI research gets pitched – ambitious timelines, small teams, funding secured on the strength of what the technology will soon be able to do rather than what it currently does.

That template recurs almost immediately. A year later, Frank Rosenblatt's Perceptron generates its own wave of confident prediction, followed by its own collapse in funding. The pattern set at Dartmouth – bold claim, institutional investment, discovery of limitations – becomes a structural feature of how AI research gets resourced, not a one-time miscalculation.

**Source:** John McCarthy, Marvin L. Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester, and Claude E. Shannon, ["A Proposal for the Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence, August 31, 1955,"](https://ojs.aaai.org/aimagazine/index.php/aimagazine/article/view/1904) reprinted in _AI Magazine_, 27(4), 2006, 12–14.
