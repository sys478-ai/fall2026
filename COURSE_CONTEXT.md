# SYS 478 Course Context

## Course

**SYS 478 – Thinking Machines, Human Choices: Power and Responsibility in AI Design and Deployment**

Senior seminar for a largely non-technical / non-CS audience.

This is **not** a machine learning or programming course. Students should understand enough technical mechanism to reason about AI systems, but the main goal is to analyze AI as a human, social, political, and ethical phenomenon.

The course meets twice per week for approximately 100 minutes.

## Core Course Goal

By the end of the course, students should be able to encounter an unfamiliar AI system and:

1. Figure out roughly what it is supposed to do and how it works.
2. Distinguish descriptive, normative, and political/institutional claims about it.
3. Analyze the system through multiple human-centered lenses.
4. Identify affected people, institutions, values, incentives, and uncertainties.
5. Make a reasoned judgment about whether and how the system should be designed, deployed, or governed.
6. Communicate that analysis clearly to a public audience.

## Recurring Analytical Lenses

The course uses four recurring lenses.

### Biological
Questions about bodies, brains, nervous systems, plasticity, embodiment, development, and biological mechanisms of learning.

### Psychological
Questions about learning, reinforcement, mental models, motivation, cognition, judgment, attention, and individual behavior.

### Social / Political
Questions about culture, language, relationships, institutions, classification, power, labor, expertise, incentives, ownership, and who gets to decide.

### Moral
Questions about values, obligations, rights, harms, fairness, care, justice, responsibility, and what ought to be done.

A central idea of the course:

> Each lens makes some features of a system visible while leaving others out.

Students should learn to compare lenses rather than search for a single “correct” lens.

## Recurring Claims Framework

Students also distinguish among three kinds of claims.

### Descriptive
Claims about what is happening, what exists, or what a system can do.

### Normative
Claims about what is good, bad, desirable, fair, appropriate, or ought to happen.

### Political / Institutional
Claims about who has authority, who decides, how resources or risks are distributed, and how institutions structure decisions.

Students should notice when these dimensions overlap.

## Recurring AI System Analysis Method

When students encounter an AI system, they should ask:

### Purpose
- What is this system supposed to do?
- What problem is it trying to solve?

### Mechanism
- What goes in?
- What is learned, calculated, or inferred?
- What comes out?
- What role do data, features, models, predictions, thresholds, or feedback play?

### Decision
- Who acts on the output?
- Is the model making a prediction, recommendation, ranking, classification, or decision?
- What happens when it is wrong?

### Human and Social Context
- Who is affected?
- Who designed or controls the system?
- What assumptions are built into it?
- What incentives or institutions shape its use?

### Lenses
- What does a biological lens reveal, if relevant?
- What does a psychological lens reveal?
- What does a social / political lens reveal?
- What does a moral lens reveal?

### Claims
- What descriptive claims are being made?
- What normative claims are being made?
- What political or institutional decisions are embedded in the system?

### Uncertainty
- What do we not know?
- What evidence would we need before making a judgment?

## Course Structure

The course is organized into seven broad modules.

### Module 1 – Being Human

Focus:
- Biological foundations of learning
- Psychological learning
- Behaviorism
- Constructivism
- Social learning
- Culture and meaning
- Moral judgment
- Power

The purpose of this module is not to survey neuroscience, psychology, sociology, and philosophy comprehensively.

Instead, students encounter several different ways of explaining human learning, intelligence, judgment, and behavior.

Key synthesis question:

> What does this lens let us notice about humans that another lens misses?

Important concepts already introduced or planned:
- neurons
- synapses
- neural networks
- plasticity
- Hebbian learning
- embodiment
- development
- behaviorism
- reinforcement
- constructivism
- Vygotsky
- social learning
- culture
- moral frameworks
- power

### Module 2 – How Machines Learn

Students should understand the conceptual pipeline:

**data → representation/features → model → objective → training → prediction → decision**

This module should remain accessible to non-programmers.

Useful activities:
- Teachable Machine classifier
- threshold-setting exercises
- simple reinforcement-learning environments
- changing data and observing model behavior
- comparing human and machine learning

Important recurring question:

> What does it mean to say that a machine has “learned”?

### Module 3 – AI in the World

Analyze deployed AI systems through concrete cases.

Potential cases:
- hiring
- predictive policing
- algorithmic risk assessment
- recommendation systems
- education
- health
- generative AI

Cases should allow students to connect technical mechanism to institutional decisions.

### Module 4 – Power / Political Economy

Focus on:
- ownership
- infrastructure
- labor
- data
- compute
- market concentration
- institutions
- incentives
- who benefits
- who bears costs
- who has authority

Avoid treating “ethics” as only individual moral decision-making.

### Module 5 – Neuromorphic Computing

This connects to the BRAID / NSF research work.

Topics may include:
- brain-inspired computing
- event-driven computation
- cerebellum-inspired architectures
- anomaly detection
- differences between conventional AI and neuromorphic systems
- claims made about biological inspiration

Potential technical concepts:
- Purkinje cells
- granule cells
- climbing fibers
- mossy fibers
- deep cerebellar nuclei
- inferior olive
- LTD / LTP
- spike timing
- synchrony
- forward models
- efference copy

The course should not require students to master neuroscience details.

The educational goal is to ask:

> What happens when engineers borrow biological metaphors, mechanisms, or architectures?

### Module 6 – Ethics and Governance

Possible frameworks:
- Markkula ethical decision-making framework
- NIST AI Risk Management Framework
- EU AI Act
- IEEE / OECD frameworks
- anticipatory governance

Students should compare governance frameworks rather than memorize them.

### Module 7 – Public Engagement

Students synthesize technical, social, political, and moral analysis into a public-facing artifact.

The goal is responsible communication under uncertainty, not advocacy for or against AI in general.

## Pedagogical Approach

The course should be active and discussion-oriented.

Avoid:
- 80–100 minute lectures
- dense disciplinary surveys
- excessive philosophical taxonomy
- highly technical ML instruction
- assigning readings simply because they are canonical
- disconnected “AI ethics topics”

Prefer:
- short framing lectures
- hands-on activities
- small-group analysis
- physical movement
- going outside where useful
- role-play
- sorting / categorization
- case autopsies
- prediction exercises
- simple experiments
- recurring worksheets
- comparison across lenses
- public-facing synthesis

A useful weekly rhythm:

### First class
Encounter a new idea.

Typical structure:
- short reading
- 15–25 minute framing
- activity or lab
- discussion
- Closing Reflection

### Second class
Use the idea.

Typical structure:
- case
- artifact
- experiment
- group analysis
- comparison
- synthesis

## Reading Philosophy

Reading load should be deliberately modest.

Aim for approximately **one substantive reading per week**, supplemented with short explainers, videos, journalism, or interactive resources.

Prefer:
- accessible journalism
- high-quality explainers
- short primary excerpts
- visually rich materials
- concrete cases

Avoid assigning long theoretical readings merely for coverage.

Students should actually read a smaller number of meaningful texts.

Potential anchors:
- BrainFacts
- Quanta Magazine
- Markkula Center for Applied Ethics
- ProPublica
- AI Now
- NIST
- selected Vygotsky / Freire excerpts
- accessible ML explainers
- public-facing neuroscience and AI resources

## Assignment Philosophy

Avoid many disconnected assignments.

Prefer a small number of accumulating analytical tasks.

Potential structure:

### 1. Being Human Synthesis

Students analyze one example of human learning, judgment, or behavior through two different lenses.

For each lens:
- What does it explain?
- What does it overlook?

Approximate length:
500–750 words or equivalent structured worksheet.

### 2. AI System Field Guide

Students analyze an existing AI system using the recurring course framework.

Questions include:
- purpose
- mechanism
- inputs / outputs
- decision
- affected stakeholders
- evidence
- values
- power
- uncertainty

### 3. Anticipatory Case Study

Developed across multiple class meetings.

Connects to BRAID research.

Students analyze a plausible emerging technology using:
- technical mechanism
- deployment context
- stakeholders
- value propositions
- risks
- uncertainty
- governance options

### 4. Public-Facing Final

Students communicate a rigorous analysis of an AI system or emerging technology to a non-specialist audience.

## BRAID / Research Integration

The course also functions as a pilot environment for anticipatory public-engagement activities around emerging AI and neuromorphic systems.

Research-oriented classroom activities should be pedagogically meaningful first.

Possible case-study structure:

1. Identify claimed value propositions.
2. Connect technical mechanisms to potential governance questions.
3. Identify deployment conditions.
4. Select plausible application domains.
5. Develop evidence-calibrated scenarios.

Students should distinguish:
- known facts
- reasonable inference
- speculation

Avoid technological determinism.

## Tone

Course materials should be:
- intellectually serious
- accessible
- concise
- curious rather than preachy
- skeptical of hype without being anti-technology
- attentive to uncertainty
- attentive to power
- inviting to non-technical students

Avoid excessive throat-clearing.

Prefer clear declarative framing.

## Terminology / Style

Use:
- “Closing Reflection” rather than “Exit Ticket”
- spaced en dashes in prose: ` – `
- “social / political” or “social and political” where appropriate
- plain language before disciplinary jargon

Do not assume students know:
- machine learning
- programming
- neuroscience
- philosophy
- political theory

Explain specialist vocabulary when it is genuinely useful.
